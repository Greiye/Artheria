The clouds above Aralon hung low—soft, wide, and full of things left unsaid.

Rain wasn’t falling yet, but the scent of it clung to the air like a promise. The wind came in long sighs, tugging at the sleeves of coats and stirring the still parts of the day.

On the crest of the salt ridge, just beyond the last terraced garden, a boy stood barefoot in the tall grass, gripping a kite with both hands.

It was homemade—painted with shaky dragon wings and held together with twine and wildflower stems. It was too small, and the frame tilted slightly left. But it flew—*sometimes—*and that was enough for him.

He squinted at the sky, then down at the town, then back to the sky.

“C’mon,” he whispered. “Just one more.”pppppppop

During this hopeful struggle a tinker was passing by chance.

Or maybe not by chance. He was a wanderer with a patched cloak, a metal leg that clicked softly on stone, and a bag full of half-working trinkets. He was the kind of person who walked softly enough not to disturb the world, but observant enough to know exactly when to stop.

“You’ll lose it in the wind,” the tinker said gently, nodding toward the sky.

The boy tightened his grip. “Not if I fly it first.”

The tinker tilted his head. “Why the rush?”

“Because when the rain starts, I won’t be allowed to fly anymore.”

“Rain doesn’t last forever.”

The boy hesitated. “Maybe not for the sky. But sometimes for people, it does.”

The tinker said nothing. Just sat down in the grass beside the boy, slow and careful, joints creaking like old gates.

The boy glanced sideways. “You ever fly one?”

The tinker shook his head. “I used to help someone else fly them.”

“Why’d you stop?”

“Because I forgot how to hold on.”

The boy considered that.

“Do you wanna try this one?” he offered. “It’s not perfect. But I made it myself.”

The tinker smiled—the kind that slips in without warning. “That’s the best kind.”

Together, they adjusted the twine, tightened the crooked wings. The boy waited for the breeze to shift. The tinker counted down.

“Three...two...one!”

The kite lifted clumsily at first, then soared like it had always known how.

It twisted in wide circles above the salt ridge, the painted dragon wings catching what little sun remained.

The boy laughed.

The tinker let out a breath he didn’t know he’d been holding.

The first raindrop touched the boy’s nose.

He didn’t flinch. Just watched the kite slowly drift downward, spiraling like a tired leaf.

When it landed, it was damp and bent, but still mostly whole.

“Thank you,” the boy said.

The tinker blinked. “For what?”

“For helping it fly. For not stopping me.”

The tinker looked at the boy for a long moment.

Then said quietly, “Thank you for reminding me it could.”

Later, when the rain softened and the boy had gone home, the tinker sat beneath the edge of an awning, watching the mist roll over the salt flats.

He reached into his bag and pulled out a kite—old, but folded neatly. The colors had faded, the string curled like a memory. He ran his fingers along the frame, quiet and careful.

Then, for the first time in years, he was compelled.

The wind had returned—just barely, just enough.

And with a quiet smile, he lifted the kite into the sky.
