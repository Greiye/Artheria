**Ssshkt-ssshkt.** Ink whispered across the parchment like wind brushing paper lanterns. Natali Bourbon sat by the arched window of the East Archive Tower, sunlight pooling at her boots, warm and amber-hued through the ash-veiled sky. Steam hissed somewhere below—a distant vent release from the caldera’s lungs. She paused and rhythmically tapped the edge of her notebook with the side of her quill as she scanned the landscape.

She was mid-sketch of an entry for her life’s work: Tales of Artheria: A Guide to Our World. On the page was the southern ridge, jagged and bold, just as it had looked that morning when the sun breached the volcanic mist. Her lines curved, shaded, and bled into little notations in the margins. Natali aimed to be as descriptive and accurate as possible in order to provide readers with an immersive experience. As if they were there.

> “The purple-tinted obsidian here mimics the silhouette of castle spires—coincidence? Recheck alignment at dusk.”
> 
> “The ridge path shifted 1.5m—ask locals if minor quakes recorded.”
> 
> “Does ash fall sweeter here?”

She smiled faintly at that last note. She always left one whimsical question per page. Her own little game.

The scent of parchment, old stone, and sulfur mist filled the air. The breeze from the open tower window tugged gently at her maps. Somewhere far below, the school bell rang—three chimes, uneven and echoing. She didn’t react. Not until the hallway outside exploded with footsteps.

“Hurry! We’ll miss it!”

“They’re early this year!”

Two students, no more than fifteen, zipped past the archive door like wildfire through dry grass—satchels bouncing, cheeks flushed. The girl wore braids that trailed behind her like streamers. The boy was already halfway down the corridor when she called, “Don’t drop the tokens this time!”

Tokens?

Natali blinked once. Closed her journal softly. Left her ink pot open. And followed.

What began as simple curiosity quickly turned into something else.

The students ducked through staff-only stairwells and abandoned hallways where vines crept through cracked stone. Natali did her best to keep pace, but her boots weren’t made for chasing children. They emerged through a back door into the outer causeways of the kingdom—and there, the air changed.

The sounds of music, bells, and laughter swept over her.

It was **Emberwatch Festival.**

Colorful ribbons crisscrossed above the walkway, fluttering with each gust of warm wind. Stalls lined the streets—vendors selling candied pyrefruit, lava cakes, kites shaped like dragons, and masks of gold and cinder-black. Children ran with sparklers as the elders lit lanterns that floated like stars into the ash-bright sky.

Natali’s eyes widened. She had forgotten this was today.

The students dashed straight through the crowd, vanishing like ghosts between the music and incense smoke.

She pushed forward to the best of her ability. Dodging the spinning flame-dancers. Tripping over a string of lanterns. At one point she nearly fell into a basin of glowing saltwater used for the ceremonial firewalk.

“Excuse me! Pardon me! Scholar coming through!” she gasped.

She caught a glimpse of the girl again—laughing, spinning once as she passed beneath a dragon-shaped kite. Then she was gone, trailing behind the boy, who was already scrambling up a narrow path lined with volcanic stone.

They were leaving the festival. Heading east. Toward the ridge.

Natali’s lungs burned. Her hair clung to her face. Her notebook had nearly spilled out of her satchel twice as it bounced of her hip. But she didn’t stop. The terrain grew rougher—less curated. There were no more ribbons here. Only wind. Ash. And a path.

Suddenly—

The students stopped. Completely. At the edge of something vast.

Natali slowed. Her boots crunched over black gravel as she finally caught up, her legs trembling, and sweat dripping off the tip of her nose.

She raised her eyes. And her breath caught.

She had expected the red glow of molten rock, the familiar sight of churning lava deep within the throat of the mountain.

But instead—

“W…w…water?”

This was not a volcano. At least, not anymore.

Before her stretched a mirror-like lake, held in the ancient bowl of the caldera. Smooth. Undisturbed. It shimmered with deep blues and smoldering violets, reflecting the streaks of the twilight sky and amethyst peaks.

Then—a glow.

Glints of light danced across the water. Amber, orange, golden. Like fireflies. Like falling stars.

No—

**Falling embers.**

She looked up and her heart rose.

There, above them—**dragons.**

Dozens. Spiraling from behind the ridge lines, through the clouds, and from vents hidden deep in the mountains. Great wings stretched and turned, catching the light. Some glowed like stoked coals. Others shimmered like sunset glass. They wove around each other, a choreography of flame and air. Not flying.

**Dancing.**

They dipped low, their reflections skimming the surface of the lake. Then rose again—wings folding, flaring, spinning into formations no cartographer could ever chart.

The girl turned slightly toward Natali, her eyes wide and glassy with wonder.

“They do this every year,” she whispered. “But only if the lake forms.”

“The what?”

The boy grinned. “Sometimes the vents shift and cool. And the rains come at the right time. And the dragons…they come back to dance. We call it **Skykindling.**”

Natali stood in silence.

Her hand drifted toward her satchel. Her fingers lifting the bag from the bottom as she gripped the mold of her journal.

She hesitated.

She let it fall back to her side.

That night, as the last dragons vanished into the ash-lit sky, Natali finally opened her book. She flipped past the page she’d marked for today’s entry.

And left it blank.

At the bottom, in small script, she later added:

> Skykindle. You have to see it for yourself.
